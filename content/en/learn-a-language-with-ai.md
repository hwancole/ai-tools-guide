The hardest part of learning a language is usually not finding grammar explanations. It's getting enough practice: someone to talk to, someone to correct your writing, and material at exactly your level. AI chatbots are good at all three. They will hold a slow conversation in Spanish at midnight, explain why your sentence sounds odd, and turn a news article into a version you can actually read. They are not a replacement for real people or a structured course, and they make mistakes, but as a daily practice partner they are hard to beat for the price (often free).

> **Quick answer:** Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini (the free plans work). Tell it your target language, your level and your goal, and ask it to act as a patient tutor. Each day, spend 10 minutes in a spoken conversation using the app's voice mode, 5 minutes writing a few sentences and asking for corrections, and 5 minutes reviewing the new words it collected for you. If you're learning English, Spanish, French, German or Portuguese, Google Translate's free **Practice** feature (in beta, as of November 2026) adds short guided listening and speaking exercises.

## What AI does well (and what it doesn't)

| AI is good for | Use something else for |
|---|---|
| Unlimited conversation practice at your level, by voice or text | Learning from real native speakers, accents and slang in context |
| Correcting your writing and explaining *why* | A structured path through grammar from zero (a course or textbook helps) |
| Simplifying real articles, songs or videos to your level | Official certificate prep alone (use the exam's own sample papers) |
| Making vocabulary lists, example sentences and quizzes | Rare words, regional expressions and very new slang, which it can get wrong |
| Role-playing situations: ordering food, a job interview, a doctor's visit | Fine pronunciation coaching (it understands you, but may not flag small errors) |

Two cautions. First, chatbots sometimes give a confident but wrong grammar rule or an expression nobody actually says. If something surprises you, ask "Is this common in everyday speech? In which countries?" and check a good dictionary. Second, AI tends to be polite and may let mistakes slide unless you ask it to correct you. The prompts below fix that.

## A simple 20-minute daily routine

Short daily sessions beat one long session a week. A routine that works for most learners:

1. **Warm-up (2 minutes):** Ask for a quick review quiz of yesterday's words (prompt 5).
2. **Speak (10 minutes):** Start voice mode and have a conversation on one topic at your level (prompt 2). Ask for corrections at the end, not after every sentence, so the conversation flows.
3. **Write (5 minutes):** Write 4 to 6 sentences about your day or the topic you just discussed and ask for corrections with explanations (prompt 3).
4. **Collect (3 minutes):** Ask the AI to list the new words and phrases from today's session, with example sentences, and copy them into your flashcard app or notebook.

Keep everything in **one chat per language**, so the AI remembers your level, your mistakes and your vocabulary from earlier sessions. If the chat gets very long, ask it for a short "learner profile" summary and paste that into a new chat.

## Step by step

### 1. Tell the AI who you are

Start a new chat in ChatGPT (chatgpt.com), Claude (claude.ai) or Gemini (gemini.google.com). Give it your target language and variety (for example, Mexican vs. Spain Spanish, or Brazilian vs. European Portuguese), your honest level, why you're learning, and what you find hard. Use prompt 1. If you don't know your level, ask it to run a short placement chat and estimate it on the CEFR scale (A1 to C2), knowing this is only a rough estimate.

### 2. Practice speaking with voice mode

Speaking is where AI helps most, because finding a patient partner is hard. All three major chatbots have a voice mode (see the table below). Tips:

- Start voice mode **in the same chat** where you set your level, so the AI keeps that context.
- Ask it to **speak slowly and use short sentences**, and to switch to your native language only if you say "help."
- If you're a beginner, you can also do the conversation by typing first, then repeat it out loud.
- Use a quiet room or headphones so it doesn't cut you off while you're searching for a word. Claude has a push-to-talk option for noisy places.

### 3. Get your writing corrected

Write a short text, such as a message to a friend, a diary entry or an email, and ask for corrections in a table: what you wrote, the corrected version, and a one-line explanation. Then rewrite the text yourself without looking. Rewriting is what makes the corrections stick.

### 4. Read and listen to real material at your level

Paste a news article, song lyrics or a YouTube transcript and ask the AI to rewrite it at your level, keep the 10 most useful words, and ask you comprehension questions (prompt 4). Then read the original. This bridges the gap between textbook sentences and how people really write.

### 5. Review what you learned

AI is good at making flashcards, fill-in-the-blank quizzes and spaced review lists from your own mistakes. Ask for a weekly review of the errors you repeat most.

## Copy-paste prompts

**1. Set up your tutor**

> You are my patient [language] tutor. My native language is [language]. My level is about [beginner / A2 / B1...]. I'm learning because [travel / work / family / exam]. I want to learn [variety, e.g. Spain Spanish / Latin American Spanish]. Speak to me mostly in [language], using vocabulary slightly above my level. When I make a mistake, note it, and at the end of each session give me a short list of my mistakes with the correct version and a brief explanation in [native language].

**2. Conversation practice (use with voice mode)**

> Let's have a conversation in [language] about [topic, e.g. my weekend / ordering at a restaurant / my job]. Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer. Keep your sentences short and speak slowly. Don't correct me during the conversation unless I ask. After about 10 exchanges, stop and give me: my 3 most important mistakes, 5 useful phrases I could have used, and one thing I did well.

**3. Correct my writing**

> Correct this [language] text. Show a table with three columns: what I wrote, the corrected version, and a short explanation in [native language]. Only fix real errors and anything a native speaker would find unnatural; don't rewrite my style. Then show the full corrected text. Here's my text: [your text]

**4. Make real content readable**

> Here is an article in [language]: [paste text]. Rewrite it for a [level] learner in about 150 words. Then list the 10 most useful words from the original with a translation and an example sentence, and ask me 5 comprehension questions. Wait for my answers before checking them.

**5. Quick vocabulary quiz**

> Quiz me on these words: [list]. Mix it up: translate from my language, translate into [language], and fill-in-the-blank sentences. Ask one question at a time, and at the end tell me which words I should review again tomorrow.

**6. Role-play a real situation**

> Role-play a situation in [language]: I'm [at a pharmacy / checking into a hotel / in a job interview], and you are the [pharmacist / receptionist / interviewer]. Use realistic, natural language for [country]. If I get stuck, give me a hint in [language] first, and only translate if I ask.

**7. Explain a grammar point simply**

> Explain [grammar point, e.g. when to use ser vs. estar] for a [level] learner in [native language]. Give a simple rule, 5 everyday example sentences, the most common mistake learners make, and then 5 practice sentences for me to complete.

## Which app to use for speaking practice

The prompts work in any chatbot. These are the voice features as of November 2026, from each company's own help pages:

| | ChatGPT | Claude | Gemini | Google Translate Practice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Voice mode in any chat | Voice mode (labeled beta) | Gemini Live | Guided listening and speaking exercises (limited beta) |
| **Free plan** | Yes, "limited voice chats"; paid plans get more | Yes, on all plans; voice counts toward your normal usage limits | Yes, in the free Gemini app | No price stated by Google; part of the Translate app |
| **Where** | iOS and Android apps, chatgpt.com; desktop apps on macOS and Windows | iOS, Android, desktop app and claude.ai | Gemini app on Android and iPhone; not in the Gemini web app | Translate app for Android and iOS, in supported countries |
| **Languages** | Many; choose under Settings > Voice > Language | Many | Many | English for speakers of 11 languages; French, German, Portuguese and Spanish for English speakers |
| **How to start** | Tap or click the voice icon in the message bar | Tap or click the sound-wave icon next to the message box | Tap **Live** at the bottom of the app | Tap **Practice**, then **Get started**, and pick your level |

A few practical notes: Gemini gives you a transcript when you end a Live session, which is useful for spotting words you didn't understand. On ChatGPT, voice usage is measured over a rolling 24-hour period, so if you hit the free limit you can continue by typing. Google Translate's Practice feature is a more structured, app-like experience with scenarios and levels, but it only covers the language pairs listed above.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Letting the AI do the work.** Asking it to write your homework or translate everything means you learn nothing. Write first, then ask for corrections.
- **Never asking for corrections.** Chatbots are polite by default. Say explicitly that you want your mistakes pointed out.
- **Trusting every rule and expression.** Double-check anything surprising in a dictionary or grammar reference, especially idioms and regional usage.
- **Only typing.** If your goal is to speak, use voice mode most days, even for five minutes.
- **Skipping real people.** Use AI to prepare, then practice with a tutor, language exchange partner or friends when you can. Real conversations are less predictable, and that's the point.
- **Sharing private information.** Don't paste other people's personal details or confidential work documents into a chatbot to "practice" with.

## FAQ

**Can you really learn a language with ChatGPT or another AI?**
AI can cover a large part of your practice: conversation, writing feedback, reading at your level and vocabulary review. Most people still progress faster when they combine it with a structured course or textbook and some practice with real people. Think of the chatbot as a tireless practice partner rather than your whole curriculum.

**Is it free?**
As of November 2026, ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all offer voice conversations on their free plans, with usage limits that vary by service. Text chats on free plans are enough for writing corrections, quizzes and grammar explanations. Google Translate's Practice feature is in limited beta and doesn't list a price.

**Which AI is best for language learning?**
Any of the big three handles the prompts above well in widely spoken languages. Pick the one you'll open every day, usually the app already on your phone. For less common languages, try the same prompt in two chatbots and compare the corrections.

**Is AI good for pronunciation?**
It's useful for practicing speaking and checking whether you're understood. It may not point out small pronunciation errors unless you ask. Ask it to "tell me which words were hard to understand," and also record yourself and compare with native audio from a dictionary or video.

**Can AI help me prepare for a language exam like DELE, DELF or IELTS?**
It can generate practice questions, correct essays and run speaking practice in the exam format. Use the official sample papers and scoring criteria from the exam's website as your reference, because the AI's scores are only estimates.

**How long should I practice each day?**
Fifteen to thirty minutes of active practice every day is a realistic target for most people. Consistency matters more than session length.
