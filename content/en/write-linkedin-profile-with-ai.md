A good LinkedIn profile does two jobs: it shows up when recruiters search for people like you, and it convinces them to message you once they land on it. AI chatbots are genuinely useful for both, because they're good at turning a messy list of what you've done into clear, searchable sentences. They are bad at one thing: knowing what's true about you. So the method is simple. You supply the facts, the AI does the wording, and you check every line before it goes live.

> **Quick answer:** Paste your resume (or a plain list of your jobs and achievements) into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, plus 2 or 3 job postings for the role you want. Ask it to pull out the keywords those postings share, then write 5 headline options, a first-person About section and 3 to 5 bullets per job, using only your facts. Pick the best parts, edit them into your own voice, and paste them into LinkedIn section by section. The free plans of all three tools are enough for this.

## What you need before you start

1. **Your resume**, as a file or as pasted text. If you don't have one, a rough list works: job titles, dates, what you did, and anything you're proud of.
2. **Two or three job postings** for the role you want next. These tell the AI which words recruiters actually use, so your profile matches their searches.
3. **Your target in one sentence**, for example "I want a junior data analyst role in healthcare" or "I'm a freelance bookkeeper looking for small-business clients."
4. **A few real results** you can talk about in an interview. Numbers only if they're true and you can explain where they came from.
5. **A sample of your own writing** (an email or a message you wrote), so the AI can match your tone.

Remove things you don't want stored by an online service before pasting: full home address, ID numbers, salary details.

## Step by step

1. **Open a new chat** in ChatGPT (chatgpt.com), Claude (claude.ai) or Gemini (gemini.google.com).
2. **Paste the material**: your resume or list, the job postings and your one-sentence target.
3. **Run the keyword prompt first** (below). Read the list and remove anything that isn't really you.
4. **Generate the headline** and pick or combine options.
5. **Generate the About section**, then ask for a shorter or warmer version until it sounds like something you'd say.
6. **Rewrite each experience entry** one job at a time.
7. **Do the honesty and tone check** further down.
8. **Paste into LinkedIn**: on your profile, use the pencil icon next to your name area to edit the headline, and the pencil icon in the About and Experience sections. LinkedIn shows a character counter while you type, so if the AI's version is too long, ask it to shorten that field.

## Copy-paste prompts

**1. Find the keywords recruiters use (do this first)**

> Here are [2/3] job postings for the role I want and my resume. List the 10 skills, tools and job titles that appear most often across the postings. For each one, tell me whether my resume shows clear evidence of it, some evidence, or none. Don't invent experience I don't have.

**2. Headline options**

> Write 5 LinkedIn headline options for me based on my resume and the keywords we just found. Target: [your one-sentence target]. Each headline should say what I do and for whom, include 1 or 2 searchable keywords near the start, and stay under 200 characters. Avoid "guru," "ninja," "passionate" and emojis. Make one option simple ("Job title | Specialty | Industry"), one focused on a result, and one written for someone changing careers if that applies.

**3. About section**

> Write my LinkedIn About section in the first person, using only facts from my resume and notes. Structure: an opening line that says what I do and who I help; 2 short paragraphs on my experience and the kind of problems I solve, with 1 or 2 concrete examples; a short list of core skills using the keywords we found; and a closing line saying what I'm open to (for example, roles, freelance work or conversations). Under 250 words, plain language, short paragraphs that read well on a phone. Match the tone of this sample of my writing: [paste sample].

**4. Experience bullets (one job at a time)**

> Rewrite this job for the Experience section of my LinkedIn profile. Job: [title, company, dates]. What I did: [paste notes or resume bullets]. Give me one sentence describing the role, then 3 to 5 bullets that start with a strong verb and show a result or impact. Use only the facts I gave you. If a bullet would be stronger with a number I didn't provide, put [add number?] instead of making one up.

**5. Career change version**

> I'm moving from [old field] to [new field]. Rewrite my headline and the first paragraph of my About section so they lead with where I'm going, then explain in one honest sentence which skills transfer, using these examples: [examples]. Don't make the change sound like an apology.

**6. Student or first job**

> I'm a [student / recent graduate] looking for [type of role]. I have little work experience. Using my coursework, projects, volunteering and part-time jobs below, write a headline and a short About section that show the skills employers in this field ask for. Humble but confident, under 180 words. [list]

**7. Make it sound like me**

> This is too polished. Rewrite it so it sounds like a real person talking: shorter sentences, fewer adjectives, no buzzwords, and keep all the facts the same.

## The honesty and tone check

Before you save each section, go through this list:

- **Every claim is true.** AI tends to upgrade "helped with" into "led" and "worked on" into "drove." Recruiters and interviewers will ask about it, and former colleagues can see your profile.
- **No invented numbers.** Search the text for percentages, money and team sizes. Delete or fix any you didn't supply.
- **Your profile matches your resume.** Job titles and dates should be consistent; mismatches look careless.
- **Buzzwords are gone.** Watch for "results-driven," "passionate," "dynamic," "synergy," "thought leader," "leveraging" and "proven track record."
- **The first lines carry the message.** LinkedIn shows only the start of the About section until someone clicks to see more, so the opening sentence should say what you do and for whom.
- **It reads like you.** Read it out loud. If you'd never say a sentence in a conversation, rewrite it.
- **Keywords appear naturally.** Using the words from job postings helps people find you, but a list of 40 keywords stuffed into a paragraph reads badly to the human who opens your profile.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one generic prompt.** "Write me a LinkedIn summary" gets the same profile everyone else has. The job postings and your real examples are what make it specific.
- **Writing in the third person.** On LinkedIn, the About section is usually written as "I," which feels more natural for a personal profile.
- **Copying the resume word for word.** Your profile can be a bit more personal than a resume: why you do the work, what kind of problems you like, what you're looking for next.
- **Forgetting the rest of the profile.** A clear photo, a correct job title, and a skills list that matches your headline matter as much as the text.
- **Never updating it.** Run the keyword prompt again when your target role changes.

## FAQ

**Is it okay to use AI to write my LinkedIn profile?**
Yes, as long as everything in it is true and you've edited it to sound like you. Using AI for wording is similar to asking a friend or a career coach to help; inventing experience is the part that causes problems.

**Can recruiters tell if a LinkedIn profile was written by AI?**
There's no reliable detector, but generic AI text is easy to spot by eye: the same buzzwords, vague claims and no concrete examples. A profile with your real projects and your own phrasing doesn't read that way.

**What's the best AI for writing a LinkedIn profile?**
ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all do this well when you give them your resume, a target role and job postings. The quality of your input matters far more than which tool you use. You can paste the same prompt into two tools and combine the best parts.

**Can I do this for free?**
Yes. As of November 2026, the free plans of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all handle this task. If you run into a file upload limit, paste your resume as text.

**How long should my LinkedIn headline and About section be?**
Keep the headline short enough to read at a glance, since it appears next to your name in search results and comments. For the About section, 150 to 300 words is plenty for most people; LinkedIn's editor shows how many characters you have left.

**Should my LinkedIn profile be the same as my resume?**
The facts (titles, dates, employers) should match. The style can differ: the profile can be more personal and should be written for whoever finds you in a search, while a resume is usually tailored to one application.

**Can AI write my profile in another language?**
Yes. Ask for the profile in the language your target employers use, and mention the country, since tone and job titles vary. LinkedIn also lets you add a profile in a second language. Have a fluent speaker check it if you aren't fluent yourself.
